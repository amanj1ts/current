1.2 British Imperial Expansion & Land Revenue Systems (NCERT Chapters 23 & 24) 5 Qs Tested in 2026

⚔️ Paramountcy & Annexation Doctrines (Haryana 2026 Tested):
  • Dual Government in Bengal (1765–1772): Introduced by Robert Clive after the Battle of Buxar (1764) and Treaty of Allahabad (1765) — Company held Diwani (revenue) and Nizamat (police/judicial) powers while responsibility rested with the puppet Nawab; abolished by Warren Hastings in 1772.
  • Subsidiary Alliance (1798): Introduced by Lord Wellesley (1798–1805); required Indian rulers to accept permanent stationing of British troops inside their territory, pay a subsidy for their maintenance, post a British Resident at court, and refrain from maintaining independent foreign relations or employing other Europeans without British permission.
    Chronology of States Accepting: Hyderabad (1798, First) $\rightarrow$ Mysore (1799) $\rightarrow$ Tanjore (1799) $\rightarrow$ Awadh (1801) $\rightarrow$ Peshwa (1802, Treaty of Bassein) $\rightarrow$ Bhonsle & Scindia (1803).
  • Doctrine of Lapse (1848–1856): Aggressively applied by Lord Dalhousie; its principal objective was to annex Indian princely states if a ruler died without a natural male heir (denying the right of succession to adopted sons without prior British sanction).
    Chronology of Annexation: Satara (1848, First) $\rightarrow$ Jaitpur & Sambalpur (1849) $\rightarrow$ Baghat (1850) $\rightarrow$ Udaipur (1852) $\rightarrow$ Jhansi (1853) $\rightarrow$ Nagpur (1854). (Note: Awadh was annexed in 1856 under Nawab Wajid Ali Shah on the pretext of "misgovernance / maladministration" based on Outram's Report).

1.3 Constitutional & Legislative Acts of British India (1773–1935) 8 Qs Tested in 2026

Act & Governor-General/Viceroy Landmark Provisions & Haryana 2026 Exam Pointers
Regulating Act of 1773
(Warren Hastings)		 First parliamentary step to regulate EIC affairs; designated Governor of Bengal as Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings) assisted by a 4-member Executive Council; subordinated Governors of Bombay and Madras; established a Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774) with Sir Elijah Impey as Chief Justice.
Pitt's India Act of 1784
(Warren Hastings)		 Distinguished between commercial and political functions of the Company. Allowed Court of Directors to manage commercial affairs, but established a 6-member Board of Control in Britain to create a dual system of governance and strengthen parliamentary supervision over the Company's civil, military, and revenue affairs in India. First called Company territories "British possessions in India".
Charter Act of 1813
(Lord Minto I / Lord Hastings)		 Officially ended/abolished the monopoly of the East India Company over trade with India (opening Indian trade to all British subjects), except for trade in Tea and trade with China. Allocated Rs. 1 Lakh annually for the promotion of modern education in India and permitted Christian missionaries to propagate religion.
Charter Act of 1833
(Lord William Bentinck)		 Final step towards centralization: designated the Governor-General of Bengal as the Governor-General of India (Lord William Bentinck became the first GG of India), vesting in him all civil and military powers. Completely ended the Company's commercial activities (including tea and China trade), making it a purely administrative body. Added a 4th Law Member (Lord Macaulay) to GG's Council and set up the First Law Commission (1834).
Charter Act of 1853
(Lord Dalhousie)		 Separated the legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's Council for the first time (6-member Indian Central Legislative Council); introduced an open competitive examination system for recruitment to Civil Services (ICS) (Macaulay Committee appointed in 1854; Satyendranath Tagore became first Indian ICS in 1863).
Government of India Act, 1858
("Act for the Good Government of India" | Lord Canning)		 Enacted in the wake of the 1857 Revolt: formally transferred administrative power over India from the East India Company to the British Crown. Abolished the Board of Control and Court of Directors (ending Dual Government); created the office of Secretary of State for India (British Cabinet member assisted by a 15-member Council of India, first held by Lord Stanley); changed the designation of Governor-General of India to Viceroy of India (Lord Canning became the first Viceroy).
Indian Councils Acts of 1861 & 1892 1861 Act: Initiated decentralization by restoring legislative powers to Bombay and Madras; recognized the Portfolio System (introduced by Lord Canning in 1859); empowered Viceroy to issue ordinances; Canning nominated 3 Indians (Raja of Banaras, Maharaja of Patiala, Sir Dinkar Rao) to his legislative council.
1892 Act: Introduced the principle of indirect election (though word 'election' was not used) and gave legislative councils the power to discuss the budget and address questions to the executive.
Indian Councils Act, 1909
(Morley-Minto Reforms)		 First introduced Separate Electorates for Muslims in India (legalizing communalism; Lord Minto came to be known as the "Father of Communal Electorate"). Appointed the first Indian member to the Viceroy's Executive Council — Satyendra Prasad Sinha (as Law Member).
Government of India Act, 1919
(Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)		 Introduced Dyarchy (Dual Rule) in the Provinces (dividing provincial subjects into Transferred and Reserved lists); introduced Bicameralism and Direct Elections at the Centre; extended communal separate electorates to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans; provided for the establishment of a Public Service Commission (set up in 1926).
Government of India Act, 1935 Abolished Dyarchy in the provinces and introduced Provincial Autonomy (and Dyarchy at the Centre); proposed an All-India Federation; divided powers into Federal, Provincial, and Concurrent Lists; established the Reserve Bank of India (1935) and a Federal Court (1937); separated Burma from India (1937).

1.4 The Revolt of 1857 — First War of Indian Independence (NCERT Chapter 28) 10 Qs Tested in 2026

🔥 Immediate Spark & Course of the Revolt (1857):
  • Immediate Cause: Introduction of the New Pattern 1853 Enfield Rifle, whose paper cartridges had to be bitten off before loading and were rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat (offending both Hindu and Muslim sepoys).
  • Mangal Pandey (29 March 1857): A young sepoy of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry (BNI) stationed at Barrackpore, who refused to use the greased cartridges and fired at his British officers (Sergeant-Major Hewson and Lt. Baugh). He was hanged on 8 April 1857.
  • Outbreak at Meerut (10 May 1857): Sepoys of the 3rd Native Cavalry at Meerut broke out in open mutiny, marched to Delhi on 11 May, and proclaimed the aged Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah II (Zafar) as Shahenshah-i-Hindustan.
  • Nature of Leadership & Local Participation: The emergence of dynamic regional leaders mobilized large peasant, artisan, and sepoy forces and unified local uprisings against the British across Northern and Central India.
📜 Aftermath & Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 Nov 1858):
  • Allahabad Durbar (1 November 1858): Lord Canning (last Governor-General and first Viceroy) formally announced Queen Victoria's Proclamation at a grand Durbar in Allahabad.
  • Transfer of Power: Ended East India Company rule and transferred the administration of India directly to the British Crown via the Government of India Act, 1858.
  • Abandonment of Annexation & Adoption Rights: The Doctrine of Lapse was abolished; British promised to respect the dignity and treaties of native princes, and Indian rulers were now allowed to pass their kingdoms to heirs, including adopted sons (while acknowledging the British Queen as Sovereign Paramount).
  • Religious Non-Interference: The Proclamation specifically emphasized that the British government would abstain from interfering in the religious beliefs, customs, and social practices of Indian subjects.
  • Military Reorganization (Peel Commission): Ratio of European to Indian soldiers was raised (1:2 in Bengal, 1:3 in Madras/Bombay), artillery was placed exclusively in European hands, and recruitment shifted to "martial races" (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans).

3.1 Foundation of INC, Moderate Phase (1885–1905) & Extremist/Swadeshi Phase (1905–1919) 19 Qs Tested in 2026

🏛️ Foundation of INC & Moderate Phase (1885–1905):
  • First Session (Dec 1885, Bombay): Held at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay (shifted from Poona due to cholera) during Viceroy Lord Dufferin's tenure.
  • Key Figures & Attendance: Founded by retired British ICS officer Allan Octavian (A.O.) Hume, who served as its first General Secretary; presided over by W.C. Bonnerjee; attended by 72 delegates.
  • Moderate Leaders: Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Surendranath Banerjee, Badruddin Tyabji, Dinshaw Wacha, M.G. Ranade.
  • Moderate Strategy: Relied on constitutional methods of peaceful petitions, prayers, memorials, resolutions, and legislative negotiations (known as "3 Ps" — Prayer, Petition, Protest).
  • Economic Critique — Drain of Wealth Theory: The intellectual cornerstone of Moderate nationalism was the formulation of the Drain of Wealth Theory by Dadabhai Naoroji (in his book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901), R.C. Dutt (Economic History of India), and Dinshaw Wacha, exposing how Britain drained India's surplus via Home Charges, salaries, pensions, and guaranteed railway interest.
🔥 Partition of Bengal (1905), Swadeshi & Extremist Phase:
  • Partition of Bengal (1905): Announced by Viceroy Lord Curzon (July 1905; came into effect on 16 October 1905, observed as Raksha Bandhan and a day of national mourning). While officially justified for administrative convenience, the primary political reason was to weaken the nerve-centre of the growing nationalist movement by dividing Bengal on communal and linguistic lines.
  • Swadeshi & Boycott Movement (1905–1911): Formally proclaimed on 7 August 1905 at Calcutta Town Hall. Core ideas included: boycott of British-made cloth and consumer goods, promoting indigenous (Swadeshi) enterprises and self-reliance (Atmashakti), encouraging national education and Indian languages (National Council of Education, 1906), and mass mobilization through festivals and Samitis (like Ashwini Kumar Datta's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti).
  • Extremist Trio ('Lal-Bal-Pal') & Aurobindo Ghosh: Led by Lala Lajpat Rai (Punjab), Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Maharashtra), and Bipin Chandra Pal (Bengal), along with Aurobindo Ghosh (who emphasized Spiritual Nationalism and edited Bande Mataram).
  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak ("Lokmanya"): Gave the immortal clarion call "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it!"; started two famous newspapers — Kesari (in Marathi) and Mahratta (in English); popularized Ganapati (1893) and Shivaji (1895) festivals; authored Gita Rahasya in Mandalay Jail.
  • Home Rule League Movement (1916): Launched during World War I on Irish lines to demand self-government by Bal Gangadhar Tilak (April 1916, Poona — covering Maharashtra excl. Bombay, Karnataka, Central Provinces, Berar) and Annie Besant (Sept 1916, Adyar/Madras — covering the rest of India, with George Arundale as organizing secretary).

🕊️ Philosophy of Satyagraha (South Africa to India): Pioneered by Mahatma Gandhi in South Africa (1893–1914) against racial registration certificates and anti-Asiatic laws (where he founded Natal Indian Congress in 1894, Phoenix Farm in 1904, Tolstoy Farm in 1910, and journal Indian Opinion), 'Satyagraha' (literally insistence on truth) refers to non-violent public resistance to injustice. Gandhi returned to India on 9 January 1915 (celebrated as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas) and transformed the national movement through mass mobilization of peasants, workers, women, and students.

1. Champaran Satyagraha (1917)
First Civil Disobedience		 Mahatma Gandhi, invited by peasant Rajkumar Shukla; joined by Rajendra Prasad, J.B. Kripalani, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parikh. Launched in Champaran district of Bihar against European indigo planters. Major Outcome: Abolition of the oppressive Tinkathia system, under which peasants were forced to cultivate indigo on $3/20\text{th}$ of their landholdings, and refund of 25% of illegal dues extracted from peasants.

First Hunger Strike		 Mahatma Gandhi, assisted by Anasuya Sarabhai (sister of mill-owner Ambalal Sarabhai). Dispute between cotton mill owners and workers over discontinuation of the Plague Bonus (workers demanded 50% wage hike; Gandhi advised 35%). Method: Gandhi first employed undertaking a fast unto death (hunger strike) as a means of protest in India, winning a 35% wage increase.

First Non-Cooperation		 Mahatma Gandhi, Vallabhbhai Patel, Indulal Yagnik. Peasants of Kheda (Gujarat) suffered severe crop failure due to drought, entitling them to revenue remission under the Revenue Code (if yield was less than 1/4th normal produce). Government secretly agreed to collect tax only from those who could afford to pay.

• Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919 — Baisakhi Day, Amritsar): Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd gathered to protest the arrest of Dr. Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal, triggering nationwide outrage.
