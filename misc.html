3. Physiography & Mountain Systems Geomorphology

Himalayan System (South to North)

  1. Outer Himalayas (Shiwalik): Average elevation 900–1,100 m; discontinuous; composed of unconsolidated sediments; transitions into Duars in WB/Assam.
  2. Middle/Lesser Himalayas (Himachal): Average elevation ~4,000 m.
    • J&K: Pir Panjal range
    • Himachal Pradesh: Dhauladhar range
    • Uttarakhand: Nag Tibba / Mussoorie ranges
    • Nepal: Mahabharat Lekh
  3. Greater Himalayas (Himadri): Average elevation ~6,000 m; continuous crystalline core; home to Mt. Everest (8,848 m) and Kanchenjunga (8,586 m, Sikkim).
  4. Trans-Himalayas: Karakoram, Ladakh, and Zanskar ranges. Holds K2 (Godwin Austen) in the Karakoram range.

Piedmont Transition Zones of Northern Plains

  • ★ PSSSB IMPBhabar: Narrow belt (8–16 km) along Shiwalik foothills composed of coarse pebbles/boulders where streams submerge/disappear.
  • ★ PSSSB IMPTarai: Marshy, wet, swampy zone (10–20 km) south of Bhabar where underground streams re-emerge.
  • Bhangar: Older alluvium on terraces above flood level; rich in calcareous deposits (Kankars).
  • Khadar: New, fertile flood-plain alluvium renewed annually by seasonal floods (known as Bet lands in Punjab).

Peninsular Plateau & Coastal Highlands

  • Aravalli Range: Relict/residual fold mountain; acts as a water divide; blocks monsoons on its windward side leaving western Rajasthan in an arid rain-shadow zone.
  • Western Ghats (Sahyadri): Continuous, high elevation (~1,200 m); elevation increases from north to south; peak: Anaimudi (2,695 m) in Annamalai Hills; second peak: Doda Betta (2,637 m) in Nilgiris.
  • Eastern Ghats: Discontinuous, breached by east-flowing rivers (average height ~600 m); peak: Jindhagada / Arma Konda (~1,680 m) & Mahendragiri (1,501 m).
  • Passes: Palghat Gap (Palakkad) connects Kerala to Tamil Nadu between Nilgiri and Annamalai hills.
  • Eastern Hills (North-to-South): Nallamala > Venukonda > Palkonda > Nagari > Javadi > Shevaroy > Panchaimalai > Sirumalai > Palani Hills.

4. Climatology, Monsoons & Atmospheric Dynamics Atmosphere

Monsoon Mechanism & Wind Reversal

  • Driving Cause: Differential heating and cooling of the landmass (low pressure in NW India/Tibetan plateau) versus the surrounding Indian Ocean (high pressure).
  • Coriolis Effect: Deflects winds to the right in the Northern Hemisphere (Ferrel’s Law). Southeast trade winds crossing the equator become the Southwest Monsoon.
  • Arabian Sea Branch: Hits the Western Ghats (causing intense orographic precipitation on the windward side while the Deccan remains in rain-shadow), sends a branch parallel to the Aravallis without lifting.
  • Bay of Bengal Branch: Deflected by the Arakan Yoma/Himalayas towards the Indo-Gangetic plain; funneled into Khasi Hills (Mawsynram: world's highest rainfall). Merges with Arabian Sea branch over the Punjab plains.
  • Retreating / Northeast Monsoon (Oct–Nov): Withdraws from NW India towards the south; picks up moisture over the Bay of Bengal, precipitating heavily on the Coromandel Coast (Tamil Nadu).

High-Altitude Winds & Tropical Phenomena

  • Subtropical Westerly Jet Stream (STWJ): Blows in the upper troposphere (9–13 km); its northward shift across the Tibetan Plateau in summer marks the sudden onset/burst of the Indian monsoon.
  • Tropical Easterly Jet Stream (TEJ): Steers monsoon depressions and maintains the monsoon trough.
  • Doldrums / ITCZ: Intertropical Convergence Zone; equatorial low-pressure trough where trade winds converge, characterized by calm air, variable breezes, and convective storms.
  • Horse Latitudes: Subtropical high-pressure belts (~30° N & S) characterized by sinking dry air.
  • El Niño: Periodic warming of sea surface temperatures in the Central and Eastern Equatorial Pacific; suppresses the Walker Circulation and weakens the Indian monsoon.
  • La Niña: Strong cooling of Eastern Pacific waters; enhances Indian monsoon precipitation.

6. Soil Orders & Indian Agriculture Soils & Crops

Soil Profiles

  • Alluvial Soils (40%+): Deposited by rivers. Rich in Potash and Lime; deficient in Nitrogen and Phosphorus.
  • Black Soil (Regur): Derived from Deccan basaltic lava flows (volcanic origin). Highly argillaceous (clayey), deep moisture-retentive, exhibits "self-ploughing" cracks upon drying; optimal for cotton (needs 210 frost-free days).
  • Red & Yellow Soils: Develop on crystalline igneous rocks in low rainfall areas; reddish tint due to wide diffusion of iron oxides.
  • Laterite Soil: Formed through intense leaching in tropical regions with high rainfall and alternating wet/dry seasons. Silica and lime wash away; leaves iron oxide and aluminum behind; suitable for cashew, tea, coffee.
  • Karewas: Lacustrine deposits in Kashmir Valley consisting of clay, silt, and moraines; uniquely suited for Zafran (saffron) cultivation.

8. Astronomy, Earth's Interior & Planetary Science Astro & Geology

Earth's Interior & Discontinuities

Mnemonic: "कोई मुझे रेड गुलाब ला दो" (CRGML)
1. Conrad: Upper Crust vs Lower Crust
2. Mohorovičić (Moho): Crust vs Mantle
3. Repetti: Upper Mantle vs Lower Mantle
4. Gutenberg: Mantle vs Outer Core
5. Lehmann: Liquid Outer Core vs Solid Inner Core

Why is Inner Core solid? Tremendous confining overburden pressure raises the iron-nickel melting point above ambient core temperatures.

Planetary Data

  • Terrestrial vs. Jovian Planets: Inner 4 (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are rocky and dense; Outer 4 (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are gas/ice giants separated by the Asteroid Belt.
  • Venus: Hottest planet due to thick runaway CO2 and H2SO4 clouds; longest rotation (~243 earth days); retrograde rotation.
  • Planetary Moons: Ganymede (Jupiter's moon) is the largest natural satellite in the solar system.
  • Apparent Retrograde Motion: Optical illusion caused by differences in relative orbital speeds when Earth overtakes an outer planet.
  • Giant Impact Hypothesis (Theia): A Mars-sized protoplanet collided with early Earth ~4.5 billion years ago, ejecting debris that accreted to form the Moon.

Space Physics & Celestial Terms

  • Blue Moon: The occurrence of a second full moon within a single calendar month.
  • Van Allen Radiation Belts: Concentric donut-shaped zones of charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetosphere. Inner belt = energetic protons; Outer belt = relativistic electrons.
  • Quasars: Quasi-stellar radio sources powered by supermassive black holes at the centers of distant active galaxies.
  • Pulsars: Highly magnetized, rapidly rotating neutron stars (stellar remnants) that emit regular electromagnetic pulses.
  • CMBR (Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation): Discovered in the 1960s; relic afterglow of the Big Bang (~13.8 billion years ago).

2. Key Indian Space Observatories & Deep Space Explorations Payloads & Physics

Aditya-L1 (India's Maiden Solar Mission)

  • Launch: September 2, 2023, via PSLV-C57 (XL configuration).
  • Orbit: Halo orbit around the Sun-Earth Lagrangian Point 1 (L1) (~1.5 million km from Earth), allowing an uninterrupted view of the Sun without eclipses.
  • Primary Objective: Study the dynamics of the Solar Corona, Chromosphere, Photosphere, Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs), and Solar Wind.
  • Key Payload Focus (MAG): The Magnetometer (MAG) measures the magnitude and direction of the Interplanetary Magnetic Field (IMF) in situ.

AstroSat (Multi-Wavelength Space Observatory)

  • Launch: 2015 via PSLV-C30 into a ~650 km Low Earth Orbit (LEO).
  • Distinction: India’s first dedicated multi-wavelength space telescope.
  • Observation Bands: Simultaneously observes celestial sources across Optical, Ultraviolet (UV), and X-ray spectral bands (0.3 to 100 keV).
  • Target Phenomena: Accretion disks of black holes, neutron stars, X-ray binaries, Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN), and Gamma-Ray Bursts (GRBs).

XPoSat (X-ray Polarimeter Satellite)

  • Launch: January 1, 2024, on PSLV-C58 into an equatorial LEO (~650 km). World's second dedicated polarimetry mission (after NASA's IXPE).
  • Payload 1 - POLIX (Polarimeter Instrument in X-rays): Developed by Raman Research Institute (RRI) with URSC. Measures degree and angle of polarization in medium X-ray band (8–30 keV).
  • Payload 2 - XSPECT (X-ray Spectroscopy and Timing): Developed by U R Rao Satellite Centre (URSC). Provides fast timing and high-spectral resolution in soft X-rays (0.8–15 keV).

LUPEX & Chandrayaan Roadmaps

  • LUPEX (Lunar Polar Exploration / Chandrayaan-5): Joint collaboration between ISRO and JAXA (Japan). Target: Lunar South Pole (permanently shadowed craters) to prospect for water-ice reserves. Division of Labor: JAXA provides the H3 rocket and lunar rover; ISRO provides the lunar lander.
  • Chandrayaan-4: Designed as an unmanned lunar sample-return mission to collect and bring lunar regolith/soil back to Earth.

Bharatiya Antariksh Station (BAS) & Gaganyaan

  • Gaganyaan: Demonstrates indigenous human spaceflight capability by sending a 3-member crew to a 400 km LEO for 3 days using the human-rated HLVM3.
  • Bharatiya Antariksh Station (BAS): Indigenous, modular space station operating in LEO (~400–600 km).
    • BAS-1 (First Module): Target launch by 2028.
    • Full Operational Assembly: Targeted by 2035.
    • Purpose: Sustained microgravity experiments, deep-space life support research, and crewed staging platform.

3. Ground Observatories, Debris Management & Telescopes Astro & Tech

MACE (Major Atmospheric Cherenkov Experiment) Telescope

  • Location: Hanle, Ladakh (~4,270 m altitude) — collocated with the Indian Astronomical Observatory.
  • Operating Principle: An earth-based high-energy gamma-ray telescope. Does not observe visible light directly.
  • Mechanism: Detects very high energy (VHE) gamma rays indirectly by collecting flashes of Cherenkov radiation emitted when gamma rays collide with atomic nuclei in the upper atmosphere, generating relativistic electron-positron cascades.

Space Debris & POEM Mechanics

  • Kessler Syndrome: Theoretical scenario where orbital debris density reaches a point where collisions cascade exponentially, rendering orbits unusable.
  • POEM (PSLV Orbital Experimental Module): Converts the spent, dead 4th stage (PS4) of the PSLV rocket into an in-orbit, stabilized research platform. Powered by solar panels wrapped around the PS4 fuel tank, Li-ion batteries, and dedicated micro-thrusters with navigation software. Prevents space debris accumulation by conducting experiments and initiating a controlled re-entry burn into Earth's atmosphere.
  • Skylab (1973): America’s first crewed orbital space station, built utilizing a modified Saturn V upper stage; proved long-duration human habitability in microgravity despite early solar shield damage.

5. Biotechnology, Genetics & Medical Sciences Biotechnology

CRISPR-Cas9 Gene Editing

  • Definition: Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats + Cas9 (CRISPR-associated endonuclease protein).
  • Working Mechanism:
    • Guide RNA (gRNA): Synthetic RNA sequence matched to the target DNA region requiring correction.
    • Cas9 Endonuclease: Functions as molecular scissors that make double-strand cuts at the specific DNA site directed by the gRNA.
  • Clinical Application: Targeted therapy for monogenic disorders like Sickle Cell Anaemia (correcting the mutation in the beta-globin gene) and Beta-Thalassemia.

DNA Structure & Chargaff’s Equivalence Rules

Base Pairing: Adenine (A) = Thymine (T) [2 Hydrogen Bonds]
Guanine (G) ≡ Cytosine (C) [3 Hydrogen Bonds]
Rule: In double-stranded DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C.
Example: If a sample is 20% Adenine → Thymine = 20% → Total (A+T) = 40% → Remaining (G+C) = 60% → Guanine = 30%, Cytosine = 30%.

Recombinant Vector Vaccines

  • Design: Utilizes a harmless, modified viral vector (e.g., non-replicating adenovirus) engineered to carry the genetic instructions for a target pathogen’s antigen (such as the viral Spike protein).
  • Immune Response: Triggers both humoral (neutralizing antibodies) and cellular (cytotoxic T-cell) immune responses without causing infection.
  • Key Challenge: Pre-existing immunity to the viral vector itself can neutralize the vehicle before it prompts an immune response against the target pathogen.

6. Emerging Digital Technologies & Advanced Materials Tech & Materials

Augmented Reality (AR) vs. Virtual Reality (VR)

  • Augmented Reality (AR): Overlays computer-generated digital information, 3D graphics, or sound onto the user's actual physical environment in real-time (e.g., heads-up displays, interactive apps).
  • Virtual Reality (VR): Completely isolates and immerses the user within an artificial, fully computer-simulated digital environment, blocking out physical surroundings.
  • Mixed Reality (MR): Allows real and virtual elements to interact dynamically.

VoLTE (Voice over Long-Term Evolution)

  • Mechanism: Transmits voice calls as digitized IP data packets over high-speed 4G LTE networks via the IP Multimedia Subsystem (IMS).
  • Advantages: Enables high-definition (HD) voice quality, faster call setup times, and simultaneous high-speed data browsing while on an active voice call without falling back to older 2G/3G circuit-switched networks.

Carbon Nanotubes (CNTs)

  • Structure: Cylindrical nanostructures consisting of rolled-up sheets of single-layer carbon atoms (graphene).
  • Single-Walled Carbon Nanotubes (SWCNTs): Exhibit tunable electrical properties; can be either metallic or semiconducting depending on their chirality (twist angle) and diameter.
  • Key Characteristics: High tensile strength (tens of times stronger than structural steel) and high thermal/electrical conductivity along the tube axis, making them ideal for nanoelectronics, biosensors, and structural composites.

NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation)

  • Regional satellite navigation system developed by ISRO covering India and a region extending up to 1,500 km around its borders.
  • NVS Series (e.g., NVS-01, NVS-02): Second-generation navigation satellites launched to incorporate indigenous Rubidium atomic clocks and the civil L1 frequency band to enhance interoperability with commercial smartphones.

2. Indian Classical & Folk Dances Dance & Traditions

Classical Dances & Attributes

  • Sattriya (Assam): Recognized as a classical dance in 2000. Founded by the 15th-century Vaishnavite saint Mahapurusha Srimanta Sankaradeva. Accompanied by Borgeet, Khol (drum), and incorporates dramatic plays known as Ankiya Naat / Bhaona.
  • Bharatanatyam (Tamil Nadu): Ancient temple dance formerly known as Sadir Attam or Dasi Attam (performed by Devadasis). Revived in the 20th century by Rukmini Devi Arundale and E. Krishna Iyer. Heavily based on Bharata Muni's Natya Shastra and Nandikeshvara's Abhinaya Darpana ("Mirror of Gestures"). Characterized by geometric postures, expressive Abhinaya, and Sanchari Bhavas.
  • Mohiniyattam (Kerala): "Dance of the Enchantress". Performed in traditional white and gold off-white attire (Kasavu). Reflects the gentle, swaying, lyrical Lasya style (associated with Goddess Parvati).
  • Kathakali (Kerala): Energetic, dramatic classical dance-drama representing the vigorous Tandava aspect (associated with Lord Shiva).
  • Manipuri Classical Dance (Raasleela): Centered around the spiritual devotion (Bhakti Rasa) of Radha and Krishna. Emphasizes delicate, soft movements without sharp ankle jerks, fluid circular swaying patterns, and modest facial expressions.

3. Indian Classical Music & Instrument Classification Musicology

Core Traditions: Hindustani vs. Carnatic

  • Hindustani Classical (North India): Influenced by Persian, Central Asian, and Arab musical traditions. Core vocal forms:
    • Dhrupad: The oldest and most austere form of Hindustani music, accompanied by the Pakhawaj (barrel drum). Starts with an elaborate, unhurried, slow Alaap.
    • Khayal: Derived from the Persian word for "imagination" or "thought". Rose to prominence in the 18th century; flexible, ornate, with rapid taans, bol-taans, and bandishes.
    • ★ PSSSB IMPTappa: Originated from the quick, erratic folk songs of camel drivers in Punjab. Known for fast, complex, bouncy melodic phrases.
    • Tarana: Fast vocal composition using rhythmic syllables (e.g., odani, tanom, yalali) rather than meaningful lyrics.
  • Carnatic Music (South India): Retained its indigenous structure without major foreign influences.
    • Pitamaha of Carnatic Music: Purandara Dasa (standardized the beginner pedagogy, Mayamalavagowla scale).
    • Carnatic Trinity: Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar, and Syama Sastri.
    • Melakarta System: 72 parent scales (Janaka ragas) formulated by Venkatamakhin in his 17th-century treatise Chaturdandi Prakasika.
    • Andhra vs. Tamil Carnatic: Andhra compositions predominantly use Telugu, prized for its vocal vowel endings and fluid gamakas.

  • Biraha (Uttar Pradesh/Bihar): Narrative folk tradition of migration, pain of separation (Viraha), and labor struggles, accompanied by metal dholaks and chimta.
  • Burra Katha (Andhra Pradesh & Telangana): Three-performer musical storytelling ballad that combines humor, social satire, and political reform messages.
  • Villu Pattu (Tamil Nadu): "Bow-Song"; narrative musical storytelling performed using a large musical bow struck with sticks to narrate religious and moral tales.
  • Zikir and Zari (Assam): Devotional folk songs composed by the 17th-century Sufi saint Azan Fakir, blending Islamic teachings with local Assamese folk melodies.
  • Barahmasa (Himachal Pradesh & North India): 12-month cyclical song cycles describing the changing seasons and women's internal emotional states of longing and separation.
  • Bhopa-Bhopi Phad Painting Performance (Rajasthan): Traveling priest-singers of Rajasthan who recite epic tales of folk deities (like Pabuji and Devnarayanji) pointing to illustrations on a painted cloth scroll (Phad).
  • Manganiyar & Langa (Rajasthan): Hereditary Muslim musician communities patronized by Rajput and Muslim elites; custodians of instruments like the Kamaicha and Sindhi Sarangi.
  • Maand (Rajasthan): Traditional semi-classical melodic style of Rajasthan that bridges regional folk storytelling with classical ragas.

2. Indus Valley Civilization & The Vedic Age Early Civilizations

Indus Valley Civilization (Bronze Age - c. 2500–1750 BCE)

  • First Urbanization of India: Characterized by fortified citadels, lower residential quarters, burnt bricks (standard ratio 4:2:1), and underground gridiron drainage systems.
  • Absence of Monumental Religious Edifices: Unlike Egypt and Mesopotamia, IVC sites lack grand temples, palaces, or royal tomb pyramids.
  • Mesopotamian Trade: Cuneiform inscriptions refer to the Harappan region as Meluhha.
    • Lapis Lazuli: Deep-blue stone procured from Shortugai (Badakhshan, Afghanistan).
    • Carnelian: Reddish-orange beads procured from Gujarat (Ratanpur).
    • Copper: Procured from the Khetri Mines (Rajasthan).
  • ★ PSSSB IMPKey Harappan Sites (Punjab & Subcontinent):
    • Ropar (Rupnagar, Punjab): Excavated by Y.D. Sharma on the banks of River Satluj; first site excavated in independent India; famous for evidence of a dog buried with a human.
    • Harappa (Montgomery/Sahiwal, Punjab - Pakistan): Excavated by Daya Ram Sahni (1921) on River Ravi; six granaries in a row, coffin burial (R-37).
    • Mohenjo-daro (R.D. Banerjee, 1922): Great Bath, Great Granary (largest structure), bronze Dancing Girl (lost-wax cast), Steatite Bearded Priest-King, and Proto-Shiva / Pashupati seal (surrounded by elephant, tiger, rhino, buffalo, and 2 deer at the feet).
    • Chanhudaro: The only Harappan city without a fortified citadel; center for bead-making.
    • Lothal (S.R. Rao): Port town on the Bhogava River with a tidal dockyard and double burials.
    • Dholavira (Kutch, Gujarat): City divided into three distinct fortified parts (Citadel, Middle Town, Lower Town); renowned for sophisticated stone water reservoirs and rainwater harvesting.

5. Medieval Sultanates, Mughals & The Maratha State Medieval Era

Tripartite Struggle for Kannauj (8th–10th Century CE)

  • Century-long struggle between three regional powers to control the imperial city of Kannauj (symbol of sovereignty formerly held by Harsha) in the fertile Gangetic plain:
    • Gurjara-Pratiharas (Western India / Malwa - Nagabhata I, Mihir Bhoja)
    • Palas (Eastern India / Bengal - Gopala, Dharmapala who founded Vikramashila)
    • Rashtrakutas (Deccan - Dantidurga, Dhruva, Govinda III)
  • Outcome: None maintained permanent singular hegemony; ended in exhaustion of all three dynasties.

Early Invasions & Delhi Sultanate

  • Mahmud of Ghazni (998–1030 CE): Conducted 17 plundering expeditions into North India to accumulate economic wealth to finance his Central Asian empire in Ghazni. Sacked the Somnath Temple in 1026 CE (16th expedition). Defeated Raja Jayapala of the Hindu Shahi dynasty at Waihind (1001 CE).
  • Muhammad Ghori: Defeated in 1178 CE at the Battle of Kayadara (near Mt. Abu, Gujarat) by Queen Regent Naiki Devi. Fought the Battles of Tarain against Prithviraj Chauhan (1st Battle 1191: Ghori defeated; 2nd Battle 1192: Ghori victorious). Defeated Jaichandra of Kannauj at the Battle of Chandawar (1194).
  • Qutb-ud-din Aibak (1206–1210 CE): Established the Mamluk (Slave) Dynasty. Nicknamed Lakh Baksh. Initiated construction of the Qutb Minar; constructed Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque (Delhi) and Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra (Ajmer). Died playing Chaugan (Polo).
  • Alauddin Khilji: Market regulations under Shahna-i-Mandi, price control system, espionage network (Munhiyans / Barids), branding of horses (Dagh), and descriptive troop rolls (Chehra). Imposed Ghari (house tax), Charai (grazing tax), and Kharaj (land tax up to 50%).

Bahmani Kingdom & Deccan Sultanates

  • Bahmani Sultanate: Founded in 1347 CE by Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah (Zafar Khan) with its capital at Gulbarga (Ahsanabad), later shifted to Bidar.
  • Muhammad Shah II: Scholarly sultan known as the "Second Aristotle" due to his intellectual and administrative pursuits.
  • The 5 Successor Deccan Sultanates:
    1. Adil Shahi of Bijapur (founded by Yusuf Adil Shah)
    2. Nizam Shahi of Ahmadnagar (founded by Malik Ahmad Nizam Shah)
    3. Qutb Shahi of Golconda (founded by Quli Qutb-ul-Mulk)
    4. Barid Shahi of Bidar (founded by Qasim Barid)
    5. Imad Shahi of Berar (founded by Fathullah Imad-ul-Mulk)
  • Battle of Talikota (1565 CE / Rakshasi-Tangadi): The united alliance of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar crushed the Vijayanagara army led by Aliya Rama Raya under nominal king Sadashiva Raya, ending the glory of Hampi.

Mughal Administration & Art

  • Akbar's Policies:
    • Ibadat Khana (1575): House of Worship at Fatehpur Sikri for theological dialogues among various faiths (Hindus, Muslims, Parsis, Christians, Jains).
    • Sulh-i-Kul: Doctrine of universal peace and religious tolerance.
    • Mansabdari System (1571): Bureaucratic-military framework based on twin ranks: Zat (personal salary tier) and Sawar (cavalry troops to maintain).
    • Zabti / Dahsala System (1580): Formulated by Raja Todar Mal; land assessed over a 10-year rolling average and categorized into Polaj (annually cultivated), Parauti (fallow 1–2 yrs), Chachar (fallow 3–4 yrs), and Banjar (uncultivated 5+ yrs).
  • Pietra Dura (Parchin Kari): Florentine-inspired inlay technique of colored semi-precious stones embedded in marble. First utilized in the Tomb of I'timad-ud-Daulah (Agra) commissioned by Nur Jahan.

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj & The Marathas

  • Ashta Pradhan: Eight-minister advisory council: Peshwa (Prime Minister), Amatya (Finance), Sachiv (Secretary), Mantri (Interior), Senapati (Commander), Sumant (Foreign), Nyayadhish (Chief Justice), and Panditrao (High Priest).
  • Influence of Malik Ambar: Prime Minister of Ahmadnagar who pioneered Deccan land revenue surveys and Bargir-giri (guerrilla tactics) that inspired Shivaji's military administration.
  • Battle of Pratapgad (1659): Shivaji eliminated the Bijapuri general Afzal Khan using concealed tiger claws (Wagh Nakh).

Constitutional & Legal Acts of the British Raj

  • Regulating Act of 1773 & Act of Settlement 1781: Established Supreme Court at Fort William, Calcutta. The 1781 Act clarified the Supreme Court's jurisdiction, shielding Company revenue actions and public servants from judicial interference.
  • Pitt's India Act of 1784: Established a System of Dual Control. Retained Court of Directors for commercial affairs and introduced the Board of Control (6 Crown commissioners) to direct civil, military, and revenue affairs.
  • Charter Act of 1813: Terminated EIC's trade monopoly in India, opening commerce to all British merchants except for Tea trade and Trade with China. Allocated ₹1 lakh annually for promoting education and allowed Christian missionaries legal entry.
  • Charter Act of 1833: Completely stripped the EIC of commercial privileges (making it purely an administrative body). Converted Governor-General of Bengal into the Governor-General of India (Lord William Bentinck). Appointed the First Law Commission under T.B. Macaulay.
  • Government of India Act 1858: Terminated Company rule; Crown assumed direct control; introduced the office of Secretary of State for India assisted by a 15-member Council, and renamed Governor-General to Viceroy (Lord Canning).
  • Indian Councils Act 1861: Restored legislative decentralization to Bombay and Madras councils, introduced portfolio system, and granted ordinance powers to the Viceroy.
  • Government of India Act 1935: Proposed an All-India Federation; granted Provincial Autonomy (abolished provincial dyarchy and introduced dyarchy at the Federal level); separated Burma from India (effective 1 April 1937).

Social Legislation & Annexation Doctrines

  • Abolition of Sati (1829): Regulation XVII enacted by Lord William Bentinck declared Sati illegal and punishable as culpable homicide; promoted through relentless advocacy by Raja Ram Mohan Roy.
  • Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856): Drafted under Dalhousie and passed under Canning, legalizing widow remarriage due to the tireless social reform efforts of Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar.
  • First Indian Factories Act (1881): Passed under Lord Ripon; prohibited employment of children under 7 years and limited work hours for ages 7–12 to 9 hours/day. Criticized because it addressed only child labor while leaving adult worker conditions unaddressed.
  • Subsidiary Alliance (1798): Perfected by Lord Wellesley. Indian rulers disbanded native armies, stationed British contingents at their own expense, and accepted a British Resident while surrendering foreign policy. First state to accept: Nizam of Hyderabad (1798), followed by Mysore and Tanjore (1799).
  • Doctrine of Lapse (1848): Enacted by Lord Dalhousie; denied adopted heirs the right to inherit sovereign princely states. Annexed Satara (1848), Sambalpur & Jaitpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1854), and Nagpur (1854). Awadh was annexed in 1856 on charges of "misgovernance" (Nawab Wajid Ali Shah deposed).

Tribal & Peasant Resistance

  • Santhal Rebellion (Hul - 1855): Led by brothers Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand, and Bhairav against British administrators, moneylenders, and landlords in the Damin-i-Koh region (Rajmahal Hills).
  • Munda Ulgulan (The Great Tumult - 1899–1900): Led by Birsa Munda in the Chotanagpur plateau against Dikus (exploitative outsiders) and landlords to restore the traditional Khuntkatti system of joint landholding. Birsa's birth anniversary (November 15) is celebrated as Janjatiya Gaurav Divas.
  • Kol Uprising (1831–1832): Led by Buddho Bhagat in Chotanagpur against British tax collectors and land alienation.
  • 1857 Uprising: Sparked by religious grievances over greased cartridges, alongside systematic pay discrimination, lack of promotion, racial abuse, and overseas deployment mandates. Leaders: Nana Saheb & Tantia Tope (Kanpur), Begum Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi), Khan Bahadur (Bareilly), Kunwar Singh (Arrah/Bihar), and Maulvi Ahmadullah (Faizabad, defeated Henry Lawrence at the Battle of Chinhat).
  • Mizo National Famine Front (MNFF): Founded by Laldenga during the catastrophic 1959 Mautam (bamboo flowering) famine, evolving into the Mizo National Front (MNF).

1. Global Trade System & Multilateral Development Banks Trade & Finance

WTO & Agricultural Subsidies Architecture

  • Genesis: General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) was signed in 1947 and took effect on 1 January 1948.
  • WTO Establishment: Replaced GATT on 1 January 1995 under the Marrakesh Agreement (signed in Morocco).
  • Headquarters: Geneva, Switzerland.
  • Subsidies Traffic Light Box System:
    • Green Box: Non-trade-distorting subsidies; allowed without WTO reduction commitments (e.g., environmental protection, agricultural R&D, decoupled direct payments).
    • Blue Box: Subsidies tied to production-limiting programs (direct payments requiring farmers to limit production volume).
    • ★ PSSSB IMPAmber Box: Trade-distorting subsidies that affect market prices and production levels directly (e.g., input subsidies, Minimum Support Price / market price support). Governed by strict reduction limits and de minimis thresholds.
  • ★ PSSSB IMPPrice Determination in India: Minimum Support Price (MSP) is recommended by the CACP (Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices); Sugarcane is governed by FRP (Fair and Remunerative Price) rather than MSP.

4. Strategic Transport Corridors & Maritime Geopolitics Connectivity

IMEC (India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor)

  • Launch: Announced through an MoU at the G20 New Delhi Summit in September 2023.
  • Participants: India, USA, UAE, Saudi Arabia, European Union, France, Germany, and Italy.
  • Twin Corridor Structure:
    1. Eastern Corridor: Connects Arabian Sea ports of India to the Arabian Gulf (UAE, Saudi Arabia).

Chabahar Port: Gateway to Afghanistan & Central Asia

  • Location: Situated in Sistan-Baluchistan Province, southeastern Iran, on the Gulf of Oman.
  • Strategic Imperative: Directly bypasses Pakistan, granting India overland access to landlocked Afghanistan and the resource-rich Central Asian republics (Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Tajikistan).
  • First Historic Shipment: India dispatched its maiden humanitarian wheat shipment to Afghanistan via Chabahar in October 2017.
  • Terminals: Comprises two primary terminals: Shahid Kalantari and Shahid Beheshti.
  • Long-Term Agreement (May 2024): India signed a landmark 10-year bilateral contract with Iran to equip and operate the Shahid Beheshti terminal. Managed on behalf of India by India Ports Global Limited (IPGL).
  • Countering China's "String of Pearls": Directly balances China's operational presence at Pakistan's Gwadar Port (located ~72 km east of Chabahar) and Sri Lanka's Hambantota Port (acquired by China on a 99-year lease).

  • Established: Inter-governmental agreement signed in September 2000 at St. Petersburg by India, Iran, and Russia; entered into force in 2002. Currently includes 13 member states.
  • Profile: A 7,200 km multimodal network linking ship, rail, and road routes from Mumbai (India) through Bandar Abbas / Chabahar (Iran), Baku (Azerbaijan), and Astrakhan to Moscow (Russia) and Northern Europe.
  • Three Alignments: Operates via Western, Central (Caspian Sea), and Eastern overland routes.

3. 1991 LPG Reforms & Industrial Policies Most Tested

Pre-1991 vs Post-1991 Architecture

Dimension Pre-1991: Inward-Looking Regime Post-1991: LPG Reformed Regime
Trade Strategy Import Substitution to protect "infant industries" via prohibitive tariffs and import quotas. Export-led integration with global trade; progressive reduction of tariffs and removal of quantitative restrictions.
Industrial Licensing Licensing Raj: Required government permits to establish, expand, or diversify capacity, causing severe supply bottlenecks. Abolished licensing for all except hazardous chemicals, alcohol, cigarettes, and industrial explosives.
State Monopolies IPR 1956 reserved commanding heights: Schedule A (17 industries exclusively State). Public sector role narrowed; disinvestment initiated via DIPAM (Ministry of Finance); State became a 'Facilitator'.

Chronology & Anatomy of the 1991 Crisis

  • Gulf War Shock: Iraq's invasion of Kuwait spiked global crude oil prices, ballooned India's import bill, and triggered a plunge in remittance inflows. By 1990/91, foreign exchange reserves dropped to ~$1.2 Billion (barely 2 weeks / fortnight of import cover).
  • IMF & World Bank Bailout: Emergency financial assistance came attached to conditional Structural Adjustment Programs (SAP) and macroeconomic stabilization requirements.
  • Two-Step Currency Devaluation: On July 1 and July 3, 1991, the Reserve Bank of India devalued the Rupee in two successive tranches (~9% then ~11%, cumulative ~18–20%) to restore export competitiveness.

Exchange Rate Architecture Evolution

  • 1. Pre-1990 (Fixed Peg): Rupee exchange rate was administratively determined and pegged strictly against a currency basket.
  • 2. 1992 - LERMS (Dual Rate): Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System: 40% of forex earnings surrendered at official government rate; 60% traded freely at market-determined rates.
  • 3. 1993-94 - Market Float & Convertibility: Unified market floating exchange rate adopted in 1993. Full Current Account Convertibility (CAC) adopted in August 1994 (Article VIII of IMF). Capital Account Convertibility remains partial and regulated (Tarapore Committee roadmaps).

4. National Income Aggregates & Macro Indicators Conceptual Math

GDP vs GNP Demystified

  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product): Monetary value of all final goods and services produced within the geographic borders/territory of India during a given accounting year, regardless of whether produced by domestic citizens or foreign multinational corporations.
  • GNP (Gross National Product): Total market value of all final goods and services produced by Nationals / Citizens of India across the world.
GNP = GDP + NFIA
NFIA (Net Factor Income from Abroad) = Factor income earned by citizens abroad – Factor income paid to foreigners within domestic territory.
Controversy Trap: If outward corporate profit repatriations by foreign MNCs exceed NRI remittances, NFIA is negative, making GDP > GNP.

Inflation & Production Indices (CPI vs WPI vs IIP)

  • CPI (Consumer Price Index): Released by NSO (MoSPI). Includes both Goods & Services. Highest weighting: Food & Beverages (~45.8%).
  • WPI (Wholesale Price Index): Released by Office of Economic Adviser (DPIIT, Min of Commerce). Excludes services completely. Highest weighting: Manufactured Products (~64.2%).
  • IIP (Index of Industrial Production): Measures real physical volume of industrial production. 8 Core Industries constitute ~40.27% weight in IIP (Refinery products, Electricity, Steel, Coal, Crude Oil, Natural Gas, Cement, Fertilizers).

Unemployment Types & Demographics Matrix

  • Disguised Unemployment: Marginal Physical Productivity of labor is zero. Excess laborers engaged in agriculture; withdrawing them causes no reduction in total agricultural output.
  • Structural Unemployment: Long-term mismatch between the technical skills demanded by employers and those possessed by the available labor supply.
  • Frictional (Search/Voluntary): Temporary transitional duration spent by workers while switching between jobs or seeking employment.
  • Jobless Growth: GDP expands primarily through capital-intensive automation and technology services, while employment generation remains stagnant.
  • Hindu Rate of Growth: Critical term coined by Prof. Raj Krishna referring to the stagnant ~3.5% annual GDP growth recorded by India between 1950 and 1980 under inward-looking socialist policies.

5. Fiscal Policy, Monetary Transmission & Taxation High Return

Monetary Policy (Reserve Bank of India)

  • CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio): Percentage of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) banks must park as cash exclusively with RBI. Earns 0% interest.
  • SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio): Percentage of NDTL banks must maintain in liquid assets (Gold, approved Government Securities, or unencumbered Cash). Earns interest yield.
  • Repo Rate: Overnight borrowing rate at which commercial banks borrow funds from the RBI against government securities collateral.
  • Bank Rate: Long-term lending rate by RBI to commercial banks without any collateral.
  • Reverse Repo Rate: Rate at which banks park surplus liquidity with the RBI, absorbing excess money from the financial system.
  • Money Multiplier: Inverse relationship with statutory reserve ratios: $\text{Money Multiplier} = \frac{1}{\text{Reserve Ratio}}$. When RBI increases CRR or SLR, the credit-creation capacity of banks contracts and the money multiplier drops.

Fiscal Policy & Deficits (Ministry of Finance)

  • Counter-Cyclical Fiscal Policy: In recessions, government boosts capex spending and cuts taxes to elevate Aggregate Demand. To curb demand-pull inflation, government curtails spending and hikes taxes.
  • Fiscal Deficit: $\text{Total Expenditure} - \text{Total Receipts excluding Borrowings}$. Measures the net annual borrowing requirement of the government.
  • FRBM Act 2003: Mandated reduction of Fiscal Deficit to 3% of GDP and elimination of Revenue Deficit. Review panel ceiling: 60% total Debt-to-GDP (40% Centre, 20% States).
  • Twin Deficit: Simultaneous existence of a large Fiscal Deficit and a high Current Account Deficit (CAD).
  • Windfall Tax: Special retrospective tax levied on unexpectedly high, supernormal corporate profits caused by external macroeconomic events (e.g. geopolitical energy price surges).
  • Laffer Curve: Theoretical curve illustrating that beyond an optimal tax rate, further increases in tax rates lead to declining total tax revenue due to evasion and disincentives.
  • Liquidity Trap: Keynesian phenomenon where nominal interest rates approach zero and money demand becomes perfectly elastic; additional monetary injections fail to lower interest rates or stimulate borrowing.

6. Schemes, Committees & Global Indices Committees & Indices

Core Economics Committees

  • Narasimham Committee I (1991) & II (1998): Banking sector reforms; recommended lowering CRR and SLR, phasing out directed credit programs, ending dual control of RBI and MoF, bank consolidations, and introducing Narrow Banking for bad assets.
  • Chelliah Committee (1991) & Kelkar Committee (2002): Formulated comprehensive blueprints for rationalizing personal income tax brackets, reducing corporate tax rates, eliminating loopholes, and designing the Value Added Tax (VAT) / Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime.
  • ★ PSSSB IMPB. Sivaraman Committee (1979): Recommended the establishment of NABARD (National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development), formed on 12 July 1982. Anchored rural credit and SHG-Bank linkage programs.
  • S.S. Tarapore Committee (1997 & 2006): Laid out strict preconditions and roadmaps for Full Capital Account Convertibility (CAC), including fiscal consolidation, low inflation, and low NPAs.

Key Welfare Schemes & Mechanisms

  • ★ PSSSB IMPPM-KISAN (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi): Central sector scheme providing ₹6,000 per year direct income support to landholding farmer families, paid in 3 equal four-monthly installments of ₹2,000.
  • ★ PSSSB IMPPM Kisan Maandhan Yojana: Old age pension scheme for small and marginal farmers (<2 hectares, 18–40 years entry age) providing an assured monthly pension of ₹3,000 after attaining 60 years of age with a 50:50 matching contribution by the Central Government.
  • ★ PSSSB IMPRevamped PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana - Kharif 2020): Scheme enrollment was made 100% voluntary for all farmers (including institutional loanee farmers); utilizes satellite remote sensing and crop-cutting experiments for rapid insurance claim settlements.
  • PLI (Production Linked Incentive) Scheme: Performance-based incentive scheme launched in 2020 across 14 manufacturing sectors with an outlay of ~₹1.97 Lakh Crore to encourage domestic production and offset inverted duty structures.
  • InVITs (Infrastructure Investment Trusts): Pooled investment vehicles regulated by SEBI that enable infrastructure developers to monetize revenue-generating long-gestation assets.
  • Project Nexus: Multilateral initiative pioneered by BIS and central banks (including RBI) to connect instant real-time retail cross-border payment platforms.
