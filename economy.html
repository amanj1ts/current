Module 1: Economic Planning, Five-Year Plans & Green Revolution High-Yield (20 PYQs in Haryana)

1.1 Pre-Independence Precursors to Planning

  • • Visvesvaraya Plan (1934): First systematic 10-year plan by M. Visvesvaraya in his book "Planned Economy for India"; focused on shifting labor from agriculture to industry.
  • • FICCI Proposal (1934): Headed by President N.R. Sarkar; called for a National Planning Commission.
  • • National Planning Committee (1938): Set up at Haripura Congress session by INC President Netaji Subhash Chandra Bose under the chairmanship of Jawaharlal Nehru (15 members).
  • • Bombay Plan (1944): Drafted by 8 top industrialists (J.R.D. Tata, Purshottamdas Thakurdas, G.D. Birla, etc.); 15-year plan with ₹10,000 crore outlay aiming to double per capita income.
  • • Gandhian Plan (1944): Drafted by Acharya Shriman Narayan Agarwal; emphasized economic decentralization, rural development, and cottage industries.
  • • People's Plan (1945): 10-year plan by M.N. Roy ( ₹15,000 crore outlay); prioritized agriculture and consumer goods via state-owned industrialization and land nationalization.
  • • Sarvodaya Plan (1950): Drafted by Jaiprakash Narayan (inspired by Gandhian Plan & Vinoba Bhave); stressed land reforms, freedom from foreign technology, and decentralized planning.

1.2 Constitutional & Institutional Framework

  • • Constitutional Status: Economic & Social Planning is in the 7th Schedule, Concurrent List (Entry 20). Borrowed from the Soviet Union (USSR); operates within a Mixed Economy model.
  • • Planning Commission (March 1950): Established by a Cabinet Resolution as an extra-constitutional, non-statutory body. Chaired ex-officio by the Prime Minister (1st Chairman: JL Nehru; 1st Deputy Chairman: Gulzarilal Nanda). Formulated FYPs prior to 2015.
  • • National Development Council (NDC): Set up on 6 August 1952 (Rashtriya Vikas Parishad). Non-constitutional/non-statutory body chaired by the PM, comprising Union Cabinet Ministers, all State CMs, UT Administrators, and PC Members to mobilize national resources for plans.
  • • NITI Aayog (1 January 2015): Replaced the Planning Commission; follows a 'Bottom-Up' approach promoting Cooperative Federalism. Has no financial allocation powers (acts as a policy think-tank).
  • • 15-Year Vision Framework (Post-2017): Replaced FYPs after the 12th Plan. Consists of a 15-Year Vision Document (2017–32), 7-Year National Development Agenda, and 3-Year Action Agenda.
  • • Types of Planning: Imperative (strict socialist state control) vs. Indicative (state acts as facilitator alongside market); Perspective Planning (long-term 15–25 yr targets).

1.4 The Green Revolution: Core Mechanics & Impact

  • • Pioneers: Global Father: American agronomist Norman Borlaug (1960s). Father of Green Revolution in India: M.S. Swaminathan (saved millions from starvation via wheat & rice productivity). Political Architect (Agri Minister): C. Subramaniam.
  • • Two Phases: Phase I (Mid-1960s to Mid-1970s) restricted to affluent wheat-growing states: Punjab, Haryana, and Western Uttar Pradesh (plus parts of Andhra Pradesh & Tamil Nadu). Phase II (Mid-1970s to Mid-1980s) spread to more states and crops.
  • • Mandatory Package Inputs: High-Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds required a combined package of chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and regular/assured water supply (irrigation), plus agricultural mechanization. It explicitly rejected reliance on traditional farming techniques.
  • • Rise in Rural Inequality (Pre-Reform): The initial surge in rural inequality following the Green Revolution was driven primarily by asset-linked access to new technology—large farmers could afford costly HYV inputs, tube-wells, and credit, whereas small/marginal farmers lacked capital.
  • • Ecological & Policy Fallout: Overuse of subsidized Urea skewed NPK ratios and degraded soil; free power subsidies caused severe groundwater depletion in Punjab & Haryana.
  • • Second & Evergreen Revolutions (2006): Second Green Revolution launched by Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam (biotechnology, genetic engineering, soil-specific seeds). Evergreen Revolution conceptualized by M.S. Swaminathan for ecologically sustainable productivity.

Module 2: 1991 Economic Reforms (LPG), Industrial & Fiscal Policy High-Yield (22 PYQs in Haryana)

2.1 The 1991 Crisis & New Economic Policy (NEP)

  • • Announcement: Announced on 24 July 1991 (under PM P.V. Narasimha Rao & FM Dr. Manmohan Singh) to pull India out of a severe Balance of Payments (BoP) crisis.
  • • Triggers of 1991 Crisis: 1990–91 Gulf/Iraq War (spiked oil prices & halted Gulf remittances), disintegration of USSR, inflation jumping to 16.7%, high fiscal deficit (interest liability hit 36.4% of govt expenditure), and forex reserves dropping to a level barely sufficient for 2 weeks of imports (forcing India to pledge 65 tons of gold).
  • • Stabilization vs. Structural Reform Measures (Crucial Exam Distinction):
    – Stabilization Measures (Short-Term): Aimed at correcting weaknesses in the Balance of Payments (external payment pressures) and controlling inflation by managing aggregate demand.
    – Structural Reform Measures (Long-Term): Aimed at improving the efficiency of the economy and increasing its international competitiveness by removing rigidities across Industrial, Trade, and Financial sectors.

2.2 Evolution of Industrial Policies (1948–1991)

  • • IPR 1948 (Shyama Prasad Mukherjee): Established India as a Mixed Economy; classified industries into 4 categories (Strategic State Monopoly, Basic/Key, Controlled Private, Private/Cooperative). Enforced via Industries (Development & Regulation) Act, 1951.
  • • IPR 1956 ("Economic Constitution of India" / "Bible of State Capitalism"): Classified industries into 3 Schedules: Schedule A (17 industries: exclusive State responsibility), Schedule B (12 industries: progressively state-owned, private supplements), and Schedule C (remaining private sector, subject to compulsory licensing).
  • • Industrial Policy 1977: Focused on cottage & small-scale industries in rural/small towns; introduced the "Tiny Sector" concept.
  • • New Industrial Policy (NIP) 1991: Ended the 'Licence, Permit and Quota Raj' to attain international competitiveness. Amended the MRTP Act to remove threshold asset limits on dominant undertakings (later replaced by Competition Act, 2002).

(Removing state controls & entry barriers)		 • Abolition of Industrial Licensing for almost all projects.

3. Globalisation
(Integrating domestic economy with world economy)		 • Core Flows (PYQ): Free movement of goods & services (trade), capital/investments (FDI & FII), technology, and labor/tourists across borders (NOT revival of barter or import quotas).
• Trade Liberalisation: Dismantling of Quantitative Restrictions (QRs/import quotas fully removed by April 2001), sharp reduction of import tariffs/customs duties, and abolition of export duties.
• FERA (1973) replaced by FEMA (1999) to 'manage' rather than 'regulate' forex. 		• Growth Driver (PYQ): Liberalization of trade and investment policies most significantly accelerated post-1991 GDP growth (especially via IT/BPO outsourcing).
• Post-1991 Rural & Agricultural Challenges (PYQ):
1. Small & Marginal Farmers: Faced higher costs of production due to reduced subsidies on fertilizers, power, and credit, plus stiff global competition.
2. Rural Banking Slowdown: Expansion of rural banking slowed post-1991 due to chronic underperformance, high NPAs, and severe loan recovery issues.

2.4 Union Budget, Deficits & Constitutional Funds

  • • Constitutional Provisions: Article 112 ('Annual Financial Statement'—word 'Budget' is absent in Constitution); Article 266(1) (Consolidated Fund of India—requires Parliamentary approval for withdrawal); Article 266(2) (Public Account—govt acts as banker, e.g., PF, no Parliamentary approval needed); Article 267 (Contingency Fund—imprest account at President's disposal).
  • • Receipts & Expenditure Matrix:
    – Revenue Receipts: Neither create liability nor reduce assets (Direct Taxes: Progressive, e.g., Income/Corporate Tax; Indirect Taxes: Regressive, e.g., GST, Customs; Non-Tax: PSU Dividends, Interest receipts, Fees/Fines).
    – Capital Receipts: Create liability (Market Borrowings/Loans) or reduce assets (Disinvestment proceeds, Recovery of loans).
    – Revenue Expenditure: Does not create assets (Salaries, Pensions, Subsidies, Interest payments, Grants).
    – Capital Expenditure: Creates physical/financial assets (Roads, Hospitals, Machinery) or reduces liabilities (Loan repayment).
  • • Deficit Formulas:
    – $\text{Revenue Deficit} = \text{Revenue Expenditure} - \text{Revenue Receipts}$
    – $\text{Gross Fiscal Deficit} = \text{Total Expenditure} - (\text{Revenue Receipts} + \text{Non-Debt Capital Receipts})$ (Equals total borrowing requirement of govt).
    – $\text{Primary Deficit} = \text{Gross Fiscal Deficit} - \text{Net Interest Liabilities}$.

2.5 Fiscal Reforms: GST (2017) & FRBM Act (2003)

  • • Goods and Services Tax (GST) (PYQ): Launched on 1 July 2017 (101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016).
    – Nature: Unified, destination-based consumption tax applicable on 'supply' side; eliminates the cascading effect (tax on tax).
    – Structure: Dual GST comprising CGST (Centre), SGST (State), UTGST, and IGST (levied by Centre on inter-state trade & imports).
    – Slabs: 4 primary slabs: 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28% (plus 3% on gold and 0.25% on rough precious stones).
    – Exclusions from GST: Petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, motor spirit (petrol), natural gas, aviation turbine fuel, alcoholic liquor for human consumption, electricity, and stamp duty remain outside GST.
  • • FRBM Act, 2003 & N.K. Singh Committee (PYQ):
    – Enacted in 2003 to institutionalize financial discipline, reduce fiscal/revenue deficits, and ensure inter-generational equity.
    – N.K. Singh Committee (Set up in 2016) (PYQ): Constituted by Govt of India in 2016 to review the FRBM Act. Recommended a glide path targeting Fiscal Deficit at 3.0% of GDP (up to March 2020), 2.8% (2020–21), and 2.5% by 2023, alongside a Debt-to-GDP ratio target of 60% (40% Centre + 20% States). (Note: N.K. Singh also chaired the 15th Finance Commission, which fixed vertical tax devolution to states at 41%).
Module 3: Money, Banking, Monetary Policy & Financial Institutions High-Yield (16 PYQs in Haryana)

3.1 Reserve Bank of India (RBI), BFS & MPC (2016)

  • • RBI Evolution: Statutory body established on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934 (Hilton Young Commission). HQ shifted from Kolkata to Mumbai in 1937. Nationalized on 1 January 1949 via RBI (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act, 1948.
  • • Core Functions (PYQ): Central authority controlling Monetary Policy in India; sole right to issue currency notes (except ₹1 notes & coins, which are issued by Ministry of Finance); Banker's Bank; Banker & Debt Manager to Govt; Custodian of Forex Reserves.
  • • Board for Financial Supervision (BFS) (PYQ): Established in November 1994 as a committee under the Central Board of Directors of RBI to undertake consolidated supervision of banks, DFIs, and NBFCs.
  • • Monetary Policy Committee (MPC, 2016) (PYQ):
    – Statutory 6-member body formed in 2016 (under Section 45ZB of amended RBI Act, 1934) on recommendations of the Urjit Patel Committee (2014).
    – Composition: 6 Members (3 from RBI including the Governor as ex-officio Chairperson + 3 external members nominated by Govt of India). Governor holds a casting vote in case of a tie.
    – Primary Function (PYQ): To set the benchmark policy Repo Rate to maintain price stability (CPI inflation target of 4% with a $\pm 2\%$ tolerance band).

3.2 Monetary Policy Tools & Money Supply

  • • Quantitative Tools (Volume of Credit):
    – Repo Rate (Policy Rate) (PYQ): Benchmark rate used by RBI for signalling its monetary policy stance and managing short-term liquidity; rate at which RBI lends short-term funds to banks against govt securities (under LAF).
    – Reverse Repo Rate: Rate at which RBI borrows/absorbs liquidity from commercial banks.
    – Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): % of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) banks must keep with RBI in cash only (earns no interest).
    – Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR): % of NDTL banks must maintain with themselves in liquid assets (cash, gold, unencumbered G-Secs) under Banking Regulation Act, 1949.
    – Marginal Standing Facility (MSF): Penal overnight borrowing window for banks (up to 1% of NDTL, dipping into SLR securities) at $\text{Repo Rate} + 1\%$.
    – Bank Rate: Long-term lending rate without collateral; serves as penalty rate.
    – Open Market Operations (OMO): Outright buying/selling of G-Secs by RBI (sells during inflation to suck liquidity; buys during deflation).
    – Trap Note (PYQ): Foreign Trade Policy (formulated by Ministry of Commerce) and Fiscal Deficit targets are NOT monetary policy tools of RBI.
  • • Money Supply Aggregates (1977): $M_0$ (Reserve/High-Powered Money = Currency in circulation + Bankers' deposits with RBI + Other deposits); $M_1$ (Narrow Money = Currency with public + Demand deposits + Other deposits with RBI); $M_3$ (Broad Money = $M_1 +$ Time deposits with banks).

3.4 Digital Payments, NPCI, RTGS & External Flows (PYQ Core)

  • • National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) (PYQ): Established in 2008 (under Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 by RBI & IBA). Principal function: To create a robust, interoperable, and technology-driven retail payment ecosystem to support digital transactions across India (operates UPI, RuPay, IMPS, FASTag, AePS, NACH).
  • • National Financial Switch (NFS) (PYQ): Operated by NPCI; it is the largest network of shared automated teller machines (ATMs) in India that allows ATMs of different banks to interconnect and work together.
  • • RTGS $24\times7\times365$ (PYQ): Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) (for high-value transactions $\ge ₹2\text{ lakh}$) has been available round-the-clock ($24\times7\times365$) in India since December 2020 (NEFT became $24\times7$ in Dec 2019).
  • • External Commercial Borrowing (ECB) (PYQ): Commercial loans raised by eligible resident Indian entities from non-resident lenders in foreign currency/INR. Primary benefit for Indian companies: It allows access to large international funds (often at cheaper interest rates) that may not be available domestically.
  • • FDI in Insurance Sector (Union Budget 2025 PYQ): The Union Budget 2025 increased the Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) limit in the insurance sector to 100% from the earlier limit of 74% (for companies investing the entire premium in India).
  • • Trade Financing by Banks (PYQ): Commercial banks play a vital role in international trade primarily by financing and facilitating import and export transactions (issuing Letters of Credit, forex dealing, discounting bills).

3.5 Banking History, Mergers & Reform Committees

  • • Historical Milestones: 1st Bank in India: Bank of Hindustan (1770); 3 Presidency Banks (Bengal 1806, Bombay 1840, Madras 1843) merged in 1921 to form Imperial Bank of India, which was nationalized on 1 July 1955 as State Bank of India (SBI) (Gorewala Committee). 1st Bank with limited liability managed by Indians: Oudh Commercial Bank (1881); 1st purely Indian bank: Punjab National Bank (1894, Lahore).
  • • Bank Nationalizations & Mergers: 14 banks nationalized on 19 July 1969 (deposits $\ge ₹50\text{ cr}$); 6 banks nationalized on 15 April 1980 (deposits $\ge ₹200\text{ cr}$). New Bank of India merged with PNB (1993). Mega-mergers (2019–2020) reduced total Public Sector Banks in India to 12 PSBs.
  • • D-SIBs ("Too Big To Fail"): RBI designates SBI, ICICI Bank, and HDFC Bank as Domestic Systemically Important Banks.
  • • Payment Banks vs Small Finance Banks (Nachiket Mor Committee, 2013): Payment Banks can accept demand deposits only (up to ₹2 lakh; earlier ₹1 lakh) and cannot issue loans or credit cards.
  • • High-Yield Committees: Narasimham I & II (1991, 1998) (Banking Reforms, 4-tier structure, SARFAESI/ARC, Narrow Banking, LAF, CAR); Goiporia (1990) & Damodaran (2011) (Customer Service; Banking Ombudsman 1995); Raja Chelliah (1991) & Vijay Kelkar (2002/2012) (Tax Reforms); Malhotra (1993) (Insurance/IRDAI); S.S. Module 4: Macroeconomics, National Income, Keynesian Theory & Microeconomics Highest Weightage (29 PYQs in Haryana)

4.1 National Income Aggregates & Formulas (PYQ Core)

  • • Historical Pioneers: 1st estimate in 1868 by Dadabhai Naoroji ( ₹20 per capita) in "Poverty and Un-British Rule in India". 1st scientific estimate by Dr. V.K.R.V. Rao (1931–32). Post-independence National Income Committee (1949) chaired by P.C. Mahalanobis.
  • • Statistical Body: National Statistical Office (NSO) under MoSPI (formed on 23 May 2019 by merging CSO and NSSO) compiles GDP. Base Year: 2011–12.
  • • Three Golden Conversion Rules:
    1. $\text{Gross} - \text{Depreciation (Consumption of Fixed Capital)} = \text{Net}$
    2. $\text{Domestic} + \text{NFIA (Net Factor Income from Abroad)} = \text{National}$
    3. $\text{Market Price (MP)} - \text{Net Indirect Taxes (Indirect Taxes} - \text{Subsidies)} = \text{Factor Cost (FC)}$
  • • Core Aggregates Tested in Haryana Exam:
    – GDP (PYQ): Total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during a financial year.
    – GNP (PYQ): $\text{GNP} = \text{GDP} + \text{NFIA}$. Exam Rule: If GDP remains unchanged and NFIA increases, GNP increases directly.
    – NDP at Factor Cost ($\text{NDP}_{\text{FC}}$) (PYQ): Defined as the sum of net value added at factor cost ($\text{NVA}_{\text{FC}}$) of all production sectors within the domestic territory ($\text{GDP}_{\text{FC}} - \text{Depreciation}$).
    – NNP at Factor Cost ($\text{NNP}_{\text{FC}}$): Known as the True / Real National Income of a country ($\text{GNP}_{\text{FC}} - \text{Depreciation}$).
    – GVA at Basic Prices: $\text{Value of Output} - \text{Intermediate Consumption}$ ($\text{GVA}_{\text{FC}} + \text{Production Taxes} - \text{Production Subsidies}$).

4.2 National Income Inclusions, Exclusions, Circular Flow & ICOR

  • • Transfer Payments (Excluded from GDP) (PYQ): Unilateral payments made without any corresponding production of goods or services in return. Examples: Old-age pensions paid by the government, unemployment allowances, scholarships, gifts, disaster relief. (Factor incomes like wages, rent, interest, profits, and retirement pensions/salaries of teachers are included in GDP).
  • • Final Goods vs. Intermediate Goods (PYQ):
    – Why Machines are Final Goods (PYQ): Capital goods like machines are treated as final goods in national income accounting because they are not used up in a single production cycle; instead, they provide productive services over many years (and constitute physical capital/investment).
    – Physical Capital vs Human Capital (PYQ): Machines, buildings, and tools are tangible Physical Capital (separable from owner), whereas skills/education acquired are intangible Human Capital (inseparable from owner).
  • • Business Saving (Undistributed Corporate Profits) (PYQ): Significant in National Income accounting because it represents internal funds (retained earnings) available to firms for corporate investment.
  • • Circular Flow of Income (Injections vs Leakages) (PYQ):
    – Injections (additions to circular flow): Investment ($I$), Government Expenditure ($G$), and Exports ($X$).
    – Leakages (withdrawals from circular flow): Savings ($S$), Taxes ($T$), and Imports ($M$).
  • • Incremental Capital-Output Ratio (ICOR) (PYQ): $\text{ICOR} = \frac{\Delta K}{\Delta Y} = \frac{\text{Investment \% of GDP}}{\text{GDP Growth Rate \%}}$. Measures extra units of capital required to produce 1 additional unit of output. A declining (lower) ICOR indicates higher efficiency/productivity of capital in generating output.

4.3 Keynesian Theory of Income, Employment & Fiscal Policy (7 PYQs in Haryana)

  • • Macroeconomics vs Microeconomics (PYQ): Macroeconomics assesses overall economic performance using aggregate price level, total output (GDP), and the overall national unemployment rate (whereas Microeconomics studies individual market prices, single-firm cost structures, or a consumer's saving decision).
  • • Deflationary Gap vs Inflationary Gap (PYQ):
    – Deflationary (Recessionary) Gap: Created when Aggregate Demand ($AD$) is less than the Aggregate Supply ($AS$) output required to maintain full employment ($AD < AS_f$), leading to underemployment equilibrium.
    – Inflationary Gap: Created when $AD > AS_f$ at full employment, causing demand-pull inflation.
  • • Cause of Involuntary Unemployment (PYQ): According to J.M. Keynes, downward rigidity (stickiness) in nominal wage rates prevents the labor market from clearing automatically in the short run, causing involuntary unemployment.
  • • Short-Run AD Increase under Price Rigidity (PYQ): In the Keynesian short run, when prices and wages are somewhat rigid (sticky) and spare capacity exists, an increase in Aggregate Demand directly results in an increase in real output and employment.
  • • Role of Investment in Macroeconomics (PYQ): Investment ($I$) is an autonomous driver that boosts Aggregate Demand via the multiplier and drives growth in the economy's productive capacity.
  • • Marginal Propensity to Consume ($\text{MPC}$) & Multiplier (PYQ):
    – $\text{MPC} = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}$ and $\text{MPS} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y}$, where $\text{MPC} + \text{MPS} = 1$.
    – Condition for $\text{MPC} = 1$ (PYQ): $\text{MPC} = 1$ when the entire additional income is consumed ($\Delta C = \Delta Y$ and $\Delta S = 0$). Conversely, $\text{MPC} = 0$ when the entire additional income is saved.
    – Investment Multiplier ($k$): $k = \frac{1}{1 - \text{MPC}} = \frac{1}{\text{MPS}}$.
  • • Tax Cuts During a Recession (PYQ): According to Keynesian theory, reducing personal income tax rates during a recession causes an increase in household disposable income ($Y_d = Y - T$), which leads to higher consumption demand ($C$) and shifts $AD$ upward.
  • • Policy Mix for Cyclical Unemployment without Inflation (PYQ): To reduce cyclical unemployment while keeping inflationary pressure under control, the optimal policy combination is targeted public spending paired with stable interest rates.

4.4 Microeconomic Theory: Costs, Isoquants & Equilibrium (PYQ Core)

  • • Fixed Cost vs. Variable Cost in the Short Run (3 PYQs):
    – Total Fixed Cost (TFC): Remains constant/unchanged in the short run regardless of the level of output produced (even at zero output) within existing capacity. Example: Rental payment for production premises/factory building, insurance, permanent staff salaries.
    – Total Variable Cost (TVC): Varies directly with output level. Examples: Payments for contract labor, raw materials/production materials, and energy/fuel directly used in production.
  • • Economies of Scope vs. Economies of Scale (PYQ):
    – Economies of Scope: Achieved when a firm reduces its average total cost by producing multiple related products together (joint production using shared inputs/facilities) rather than separately.
    – Economies of Scale: Achieved when expanding the output scale of a single product lowers long-run average cost per unit.
  • • Types of Isoquants & Slope (MRTS) (PYQ):
    – Linear Isoquant (PYQ): Has a constant slope ($\text{MRTS}_{LK}$ is constant); occurs when inputs (labor and capital) are perfect substitutes.
    – L-Shaped (Leontief) Isoquant: Inputs are perfect complements (used in fixed proportions; zero substitutability).
    – Convex Isoquant (Cobb-Douglas): Smoothly bowed toward origin due to diminishing Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution (MRTS).
  • • Perfectly Competitive Market Equilibrium (PYQ): When a perfectly competitive market is in equilibrium ($Q_d = Q_s$), it necessarily follows that consumers' buying plans and firms' selling plans match completely (zero excess demand or excess supply).

4.5 Economic Systems, Poverty, Unemployment & Global Orgs

  • • Classification of Economic Systems (4 PYQs):
    – Command / Centrally Planned / Socialist Economy: Government controls and plans all economic activities (production and distribution of goods/services) via state ownership.
    – Market / Capitalist Economy: Private enterprise controls production; resource allocation driven solely by the price mechanism (demand & supply) for profit.
    – Mixed Economy (India): Combines both market forces (price mechanism) and government intervention/planning; public and private sectors coexist.
  • • Poverty & Inequality Metrics:
    – Poverty in India is measured via household consumption expenditure (surveyed by NSSO), not income.
    – Committees: Dandekar & Rath (1971: 2250 kcal); Y.K. Alagh (1979) (2400 kcal Rural / 2100 kcal Urban); Lakdawala (1989) (State-specific lines via CPI-IW & CPI-AL); Suresh Tendulkar (2005/2009) (Mixed Recall Period, 21.9% poor in 2011–12); C. Rangarajan (2012/2014) (29.6% poor).
    – Lorenz Curve (1905) & Gini Coefficient (1912): Measure income inequality ($0 = \text{perfect equality}$, $1 = \text{maximal inequality}$).
    – HDI (1990, UNDP - Mahbub-ul-Haq & Amartya Sen): Composite of Life Expectancy, Education, and Per Capita GNI (PPP \$).
  • • Unemployment Types: Disguised (Marginal productivity of labor $= 0$; common in Indian agriculture); Seasonal (Agri); Structural (skill mismatch); Cyclical (recession/business cycle); Frictional (temporary time lag while switching jobs).
  • • International Orgs: Bretton Woods Twins (1944, Washington DC): IMF (190+ members, SDR basket: USD, Euro, RMB, Yen, GBP) & World Bank (IBRD + IDA + IFC + MIGA + ICSID; India is NOT a member of ICSID). WTO (1 Jan 1995, Geneva): Marrakesh Agreement (1994), succeeded GATT (1947). ADB (1966, Manila); NDB/BRICS Bank (2014, Shanghai); AIIB (2016, Beijing).
